Classical Mechanics - Motion and Forces

The study of motion, forces, and energy in physical systems. Classical mechanics describes how objects move under the influence of forces and forms the foundation of physics.

Overview

Mechanics explains how and why things move, from a rolling ball to a launched rocket. It starts with a simple idea: forces change motion.

Key Concepts
  • Velocity and acceleration: speed with direction, and how quickly it changes
  • Newton's laws: objects keep moving unless a force acts; F = ma; every action has an equal and opposite reaction
  • Momentum: mass times velocity, conserved in collisions
  • Energy: kinetic and potential energy trade places but the total stays constant
Quick Facts
1687 Newton published the Principia9.8 m/s² acceleration of falling objects on EarthF = ma force equals mass times acceleration
Detailed Notes

A bigger force or a smaller mass means more acceleration. A projectile follows a curved path because gravity pulls it down while it keeps moving forward at a steady horizontal speed.

In a Newton's cradle, momentum and energy pass through the balls in turn. Simple machines such as a pulley trade distance for force: more rope segments mean less pull is needed.

Thermodynamics - Heat and Energy Transfer

The study of heat, temperature, and energy transfer, and how these affect matter. Thermodynamics governs the behavior of systems in terms of energy flow and transformation.

Overview

Thermodynamics studies how heat and energy move and change form, which is the science behind engines, fridges and weather.

Key Concepts
  • Temperature vs heat: temperature measures average particle motion; heat is energy in transit
  • First law: energy is never created or destroyed
  • Second law: disorder (entropy) tends to increase
  • Heat transfer: conduction, convection and radiation
Quick Facts
−273.15 °C absolute zero, the lowest possible temperature100 °C water boils at sea levelBelow 100% efficiency of any real heat engine
Detailed Notes

A heat engine takes in heat from a hot source, turns part of it into useful work and releases the rest to a cooler place. That wasted share is why no engine is perfectly efficient.

Heat always flows from hot to cold by itself. Making heat flow the other way, as a refrigerator does, needs extra work.

Key Concepts:
  • Laws of thermodynamics
  • Heat transfer mechanisms
  • Thermodynamic systems and processes
  • Entropy and disorder

Electromagnetism - Electric and Magnetic Phenomena

The study of electric and magnetic fields, their interactions, and electromagnetic waves. This includes electricity, magnetism, and electromagnetic radiation.

Overview

Electricity and magnetism are two sides of one force. Together they power lights, motors, phones and the light we see.

Key Concepts
  • Electric charge: positive and negative; like charges repel, opposites attract
  • Electric field: the region where a charge feels a force
  • Magnetic field: created by moving charges and magnets
  • Electromagnetic waves: travelling electric and magnetic fields, including light
Quick Facts
299,792,458 m/s speed of light in a vacuum1831 Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction1860s Maxwell united electricity, magnetism and light
Detailed Notes

Field lines show the direction of the force: they leave positive charges and enter negative ones. The closer the lines, the stronger the field.

A changing magnetic field creates an electric current, which is how generators work. A changing electric field creates a magnetic field, and together they form waves such as radio, visible light and X-rays.

Modern Applications

Electromagnetism forms the basis for modern technology including electric motors, generators, transformers, wireless communication, and medical imaging devices like MRI machines.

Optics - The Study of Light

The branch of physics that studies the behavior and properties of light, including its interactions with matter and the construction of instruments that use or detect it.

Overview

Optics explains how light travels, bends and forms images, from rainbows to cameras and eyeglasses.

Key Concepts
  • Reflection: light bouncing off a surface
  • Refraction: light bending as it changes material
  • Lenses: curved glass that focuses or spreads light
  • Diffraction: light spreading around edges and openings
Quick Facts
400–700 nm wavelength range of visible light~8 min for sunlight to reach EarthRainbow made by refraction and reflection in raindrops
Detailed Notes

Converging (convex) lenses bring light to a focus and are used in magnifiers, cameras and your eye. Diverging (concave) lenses spread light out and are used to correct short-sightedness.

White light is a mix of colours. Because each colour bends by a slightly different amount, a prism or water droplet separates them.

Modern Physics - Beyond Classical Physics

Encompasses relativity, quantum mechanics, nuclear physics, particle physics, and cosmology - the physics developed in the 20th century that revolutionized our understanding of the universe.

Overview

Around 1900, experiments revealed that very fast and very small things do not follow everyday rules. Relativity and quantum theory were the answer.

Key Concepts
  • Relativity: space and time depend on motion and gravity
  • Quantum: energy comes in tiny packets called quanta
  • Wave-particle duality: light and matter act as both waves and particles
  • Orbitals: electrons occupy probability clouds, not fixed paths
Quick Facts
1900 Planck proposed quanta1905 Einstein's special relativity and E = mc²Probability is central to quantum predictions
Detailed Notes

In the quantum atom, electrons exist in regions called orbitals where they are likely to be found. Each orbital holds a set amount of energy, and electrons jump between them by absorbing or releasing light.

Relativity shows that mass and energy are linked and that moving clocks run slow. These effects are tiny in daily life, but GPS satellites must correct for them.

Major Theories:
  • Special and General Relativity
  • Quantum Mechanics
  • Nuclear Physics
  • Particle Physics
  • Cosmology

Wave Phenomena and Sound

The study of mechanical waves, sound waves, wave interference, diffraction, and resonance phenomena. This includes both sound (acoustics) and other wave phenomena.

Overview

Waves carry energy without carrying matter along. Sound, light and water ripples all follow the same wave rules.

Key Concepts
  • Wavelength, frequency, amplitude: length, repeats per second and height of a wave
  • Interference: waves add up or cancel out
  • Resonance: a strong response at a natural frequency
  • Doppler effect: pitch shifts when the source moves
Quick Facts
343 m/s speed of sound in air at 20 °C20 Hz–20 kHz typical human hearing rangev = fλ wave speed equals frequency times wavelength
Detailed Notes

When two waves meet, crest-on-crest makes a bigger wave (constructive interference) and crest-on-trough cancels it (destructive interference). Noise-cancelling headphones use this to reduce sound.

Sound is a mechanical wave, so it needs a medium and cannot travel through empty space.

Nuclear Structure and Reactions

The study of atomic nuclei, their components and interactions, nuclear reactions, radioactivity, and nuclear energy applications.

Overview

The nucleus at the centre of every atom holds nearly all its mass and can release huge amounts of energy.

Key Concepts
  • Protons and neutrons: particles that make up the nucleus
  • Radioactive decay: unstable nuclei release radiation
  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits
  • Fusion: light nuclei join, powering the Sun
Quick Facts
~1/100,000 nucleus size compared with the atom1938 fission was discoveredE = mc² links lost mass to released energy
Detailed Notes

A nuclear reactor controls a chain reaction: neutrons split uranium nuclei, which release heat and more neutrons. Control rods absorb neutrons to keep the rate steady.

Half-life is the time for half of a radioactive sample to decay. It ranges from fractions of a second to billions of years.

Applications in Medicine and Energy

Nuclear physics has applications in medical imaging (PET scans), cancer treatment (radiation therapy), and as a source of clean energy through nuclear power plants.

Behavior of Fluids

The study of fluids (liquids and gases) and the forces on them. Includes fluid statics (hydrostatics) and fluid dynamics, describing flow behavior.

Overview

Fluids are liquids and gases. They flow, push in all directions and can lift planes and float ships.

Key Concepts
  • Pressure: force spread over an area
  • Buoyancy: the upward push of a fluid
  • Bernoulli's principle: faster flow means lower pressure
  • Viscosity: a fluid's resistance to flow
Quick Facts
101,325 Pa average air pressure at sea level3rd century BC Archimedes explained buoyancy1 litre of water has a mass of about 1 kg
Detailed Notes

An object floats when it displaces fluid weighing as much as the object itself. Steel ships float because their hollow shape displaces a large volume of water.

An aircraft wing is shaped so air moves faster over the top. Together with the angle of the wing, this produces the lift that holds the plane up.

Key Principles:
  • Bernoulli's principle
  • Continuity equation
  • Archimedes' principle
  • Viscosity and turbulence

Physics of Solids

The study of rigid matter, or solids, through methods such as quantum mechanics, crystallography, and electromagnetism. This forms the basis for semiconductor physics and materials science.

Overview

Solid-state physics explains why materials are hard, shiny, conductive or brittle, and underlies all modern electronics.

Key Concepts
  • Crystal lattice: atoms arranged in a repeating pattern
  • Conductors and insulators: materials that do or do not carry current
  • Semiconductors: materials whose conductivity can be controlled
  • Elasticity: a solid's ability to spring back
Quick Facts
14 basic 3D crystal lattice typesSilicon is the base of most computer chipsCarbon forms both diamond and graphite
Detailed Notes

The way atoms are arranged changes properties dramatically. Diamond's rigid 3D network makes it extremely hard, while graphite's sheets slide over each other.

Adding small amounts of other elements to silicon changes how it conducts electricity. This lets engineers build transistors, the switches inside every chip.

Physics of the Universe

The application of physics principles to astronomical objects and phenomena. Includes stellar physics, galactic structure, black holes, and the evolution of the universe.

Overview

Astrophysics applies physics to planets, stars and galaxies. Gravity shapes almost everything we see in space.

Key Concepts
  • Gravity: the attraction between masses that holds orbits together
  • Stellar life cycle: stars are born, shine and die
  • Black holes: regions where gravity traps even light
  • Expanding universe: space itself is stretching
Quick Facts
~13.8 billion years since the Big Bang~30 km/s Earth's orbital speed around the Sun99.8% of the Solar System's mass is the Sun
Detailed Notes

Planets stay in orbit because their sideways motion balances the Sun's pull. Closer planets move faster, which is why Mercury's year is only 88 days.

Stars shine by nuclear fusion. When massive stars run out of fuel they can collapse into neutron stars or black holes.

Recent Discoveries

Gravitational wave detection, black hole imaging, exoplanet discovery, and dark matter/energy research are revolutionizing our understanding of the cosmos.
